Where I've Worked

Experience

Grainger
Supply Chain Analyst
May 2025 – Present · MRO Distribution · Texas
↓18% Past-Due POs ↑22% Forecast Visibility ↓35% Report Cycle

Every morning at Grainger starts the same way: open Power BI, scan the overnight KPI refresh, and look for red flags. With 1.5 million+ MRO products flowing through global distribution centers, branches, and supplier networks, even a 1% anomaly in past-due purchase orders means thousands of line items at risk.

When I joined, the past-due PO rate was running at ~11.5% of open lines. I redesigned the exception reporting from scratch — building a tiered aging report in Power BI that categorized POs by severity (1–3 days, 4–7 days, 8+ days) and establishing a structured escalation cadence. Purchasing coordinators handled the short-aged items; managers got the 4–7 day cases; sourcing leads owned anything 8+ days. Within 6 months, the rate dropped to ~9.4% — an 18% reduction.

The bigger challenge was visibility. Demand forecasts lived in one Excel file, inventory levels in SAP, PO status in another system, deployment plans in yet another tool. I wrote SQL queries to consolidate these into a unified data model and built interactive Power BI dashboards that let planners drill from forecast → inventory → PO → deployment at the SKU/DC level. The result: a 22% improvement in forecast-to-purchase visibility, measured by a reduction in "blind spot" incidents where gaps were discovered reactively.

I also inherited a portfolio of legacy reports that took 4–6 hours each to produce manually. By troubleshooting broken SQL joins, optimizing WHERE clauses, and migrating Tableau workbooks to Power BI, I cut the average report cycle from ~5 hours to ~3.25 hours — a 35% reduction, freeing up 10–12 analyst-hours per week for higher-value work.

Kraft Heinz
Purchasing Analyst
2021 – 2023 · FMCG · Food & Beverage
$1.4M+ Pipeline ↓25% Cycle Time 70% PR→PO Auto

At Kraft Heinz, the day started at 7 AM with SAP. The overnight MRP run had already processed production schedules, inventory levels, and lead times to generate a fresh batch of 40–80 purchase requisitions — covering everything from tomato paste and vinegar to glass bottles, caps, and shrink-wrap film. My job was to turn those requisitions into purchase orders before the production floor needed them.

The problem when I arrived: only 40% of PRs converted automatically. The rest required manual intervention because vendor parameters were stale — wrong lead times, incorrect MOQs, expired pricing agreements. I spent three weeks auditing material master records for all 300+ SKUs, confirming lead times directly with each supplier, correcting MOQs, and updating pricing info records. After the cleanup, 70% of PRs converted to POs automatically, saving 8–10 hours of manual processing per week.

The most intense moment came during a peak season crisis: a key tomato paste supplier notified me 10 days before a major delivery that they could only fulfill 60% of the order due to a poor harvest. That ingredient was critical for ketchup and pasta sauces — 35% of the plant's monthly output. Within 48 hours, I activated the backup supplier list, issued emergency RFQs, and secured a commitment from a secondary supplier at a 7% price premium. I coordinated with the production planner to resequence the line — running pasta sauce first while the supplementary shipment was in transit. Zero production downtime.

I also resolved a backlog of 25+ invoices stuck in three-way match failures, delaying supplier payments by 15–20 days. Root cause analysis revealed 40% were GRN posting delays, 35% were pricing mismatches, and 25% were quantity variances. I implemented daily GRN reminders, a monthly price reconciliation checklist, and a 2% tolerance threshold for auto-clearing minor quantity variances. Within 6 weeks, the backlog cleared and recurring failures dropped by 60%.
